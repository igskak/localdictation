# Phase 3 — risk engine measurement

`docs/PHASE_3.md` requires three numbers, per language profile:

- **Recall of real critical errors** — of the errors the corpus proves are
  there, how many did the engine mark?
- **False-warning density** — marks per hundred words on correct text.
- **Semantic preservation after cleanup** — cleanup must never change meaning.

The harness extends the Phase 2 one rather than inventing a second: same
corpus, same `TextNormalizer`, same alignment. Two harnesses would eventually
disagree, and the disagreement would be invisible.

## The two halves need different things

| Measurement | Needs a speech engine? | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| False-warning density | No | The question is "how often does this mark text that is already correct". Feeding the reference sentences in as the hypothesis answers it exactly, and deterministically. |
| Semantic preservation | No | Cleanup is a pure function of text. |
| Recall | Yes | You cannot measure catching real errors without real errors. |

## How to run

The model-free half needs only the corpus manifest:

```sh
xcodebuild test -project LocalDictation.xcodeproj -scheme LocalDictation -destination 'platform=macOS,arch=arm64' -only-testing:LocalDictationTests/RiskBenchmarkTests/testRunAgainstTheInstalledCorpusIfPresent
```

The recall half needs audio and the engine. The `TEST_RUNNER_` prefix is
required, not decoration — `xcodebuild` strips it and injects the rest; without
it the variable never arrives and the run silently measures the reference text
instead:

```sh
TEST_RUNNER_BENCHMARK_ENGINE=whisperkit xcodebuild test -project LocalDictation.xcodeproj -scheme LocalDictation -destination 'platform=macOS,arch=arm64' -only-testing:LocalDictationTests/RiskBenchmarkTests/testRunAgainstTheInstalledCorpusIfPresent
```

Reports land in `Benchmark/risk-<engine>.md`, which is git-ignored.

## Corpus

The same TTS smoke corpus Phase 2 used: 24 samples, six per language,
generated by `python3 Tools/make_smoke_corpus.py`. **Its sentences were written
to carry an amount, a date, a name, and a negation each** — that is what makes
it useful for recall and what makes its false-warning density an upper bound
rather than a typical figure. Ordinary prose contains far fewer critical
tokens per hundred words.

Synthesized speech is also unnaturally clean and evenly paced. These numbers
say the engine works and behaves as designed; they do not say how it behaves on
real dictation in a real room.

## A Phase 2 number moved slightly, on purpose

`CriticalTokens` was promoted out of `Benchmark/` into
`LocalDictation/Services/Risk/`, as `docs/PHASE_3.md` requires, so that the
definition selecting what the benchmark *scores* is the same one selecting what
the risk engine *marks*. Two definitions would eventually diverge, and the
divergence would be invisible: the engine would be marking one thing while the
measurement graded another.

Promoting it also widened it — spelled-out hundreds and thousands in all four
languages, more currency words, month names, and the ordinals used to speak a
day of the month. The Phase 2 numeric error rate in `docs/PHASE_2_BENCHMARK.md`
was measured against the narrower set and is not directly comparable to a rate
measured after this change. Nothing was removed, so the selection is a strict
superset.

## Results

### Half one — on text that is already correct

No speech engine. The hypothesis *is* the reference, so every mark is by
definition a false warning, and cleanup runs on perfectly-formed sentences.

| Language | Samples | False warnings / 100 words | Semantic preservation | Review shown |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| German | 6 | 26.8 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| English | 6 | 42.9 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| Russian | 6 | 42.4 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| Ukrainian | 6 | 39.4 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| **Overall** | 24 | 37.6 | 100.0% | 100.0% |

| Signal | Marks on correct text |
| --- | ---: |
| number | 18 |
| date | 16 |
| currency | 12 |
| entity | 10 |

**Semantic preservation is 100% in all four languages.** Cleanup changed no
word in any sample. That is the criterion that must not move: anything below
100% is cleanup changing what the user said, and is a defect rather than a
tuning question.

**The false-warning density is high, and the corpus is why.** Every sentence in
it was written to carry an amount, a date, and a name — that is what makes it
useful for recall. On such text a signal that marks amounts, dates, and names
*should* mark roughly a third of the words. The number to watch is not its
absolute value but its shape:

- No signal fires that has no business firing. Every mark falls into one of the
  four categories the product promises to protect. There is no cleanup, no
  language-switch, and no confidence noise in this column at all.
- **German is the lowest of the four (26.8), and that is the entity keying
  working.** The capitalization heuristic is switched off in German because
  every noun is capitalized there; the three languages where it does run pay
  about 13 extra marks per hundred words for it.
- The 100% review rate is an artefact of the same corpus property. Twenty-four
  out of twenty-four sentences contain something the product exists to show
  the user, so twenty-four reviews is the correct answer for this corpus and
  says nothing about ordinary prose.

What this measurement cannot tell us is the rate on real dictated text, where
critical tokens are far sparser. That needs a corpus of ordinary work text,
which does not exist yet and is the honest next step for the false-warning
budget.

### Half two — on what WhisperKit actually produced

`TEST_RUNNER_BENCHMARK_ENGINE=whisperkit`, Whisper large-v3 turbo, all 24
samples scored and no failures — including German, which the Phase 2 run could
not score because the machine ran out of disk space mid-download.

| Language | Samples | Critical errors | Recall | Dropped (unmarkable) | False warnings / 100 words | Semantic preservation | Review shown |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| German | 6 | 3 | 100.0% | 0 | 19.5 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| English | 6 | 5 | 100.0% | 3 | 28.2 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| Russian | 6 | 3 | 100.0% | 2 | 29.0 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| Ukrainian | 6 | 3 | 100.0% | 1 | 25.8 | 100.0% | 100.0% |
| **Overall** | 24 | 14 | 100.0% | 6 | 25.4 | 100.0% | 100.0% |

| Signal | Marks on correct text |
| --- | ---: |
| currency | 11 |
| entity | 10 |
| date | 8 |
| number | 7 |

#### Read the recall column carefully

**100% recall is close to tautological here, and presenting it as an
achievement would be dishonest.** A "critical error" is counted when a wrong
word is a number, an amount, or a date — in the reference or in what the engine
wrote. The number signal marks exactly those words. So for an error to escape
it, the engine would have to replace a critical word with something that is
neither critical nor capitalized, and none of the fourteen errors here did that.

What the number does prove is narrower, and still worth having: the rule fires
on the real output of a real engine, the spans land on the right characters
after cleanup has moved them, and nothing in the pipeline between the raw
transcript and the mark drops an error on the floor. That is what could have
been broken and is not.

#### The number that actually matters is the sixth column

**Six critical words were dropped entirely** — three in English, two in Russian,
one in Ukrainian — against fourteen that were misrecognized. Roughly **30% of
this corpus's critical failures are structurally invisible to any risk engine**:
the word is not in the text, so no rule can mark it and no confidence score
attaches to a token that was never emitted.

That is the class `docs/PHASE_3.md` decided not to chase, and this is the first
measurement putting a size on it. The decision stands — the reasoning there is
about *which* errors a person can catch by reading, not about how many there
are — but the figure belongs next to the recall column, because a reader who
sees only "100%" would conclude the engine catches everything, and it does not.

Two caveats on the six: this is synthesized speech, and the corpus is small.
Whether real dictation drops words at a similar rate is unmeasured.

#### False-warning density falls on engine output

25.4 against 37.6 on the reference text, and it falls for an uninteresting
reason: Whisper writes amounts and dates in forms the rules do not always
recognize (`1.450` for `1450`, a spelled-out ordinal for a digit), so some
critical words stop being marked because they stopped looking critical. That is
a *worse* outcome wearing a better-looking number. It is the same formatting
mismatch `docs/PHASE_2_VERIFICATION.md` warned about, now visible from the
other side.

German is again the lowest of the four (19.5), for the same reason as before:
the capitalization heuristic does not run there.

## What remains unmeasured

- **The false-warning rate on ordinary work text.** Every sentence in this
  corpus was built to carry a critical token. The rate on the email, ticket
  comments, and documentation this product is actually for is unknown, and it
  is the number that decides whether the review step earns its interruption.
  This needs a corpus of ordinary text, which does not exist yet.
- **Real speech.** Everything here is TTS: unnaturally clean, evenly paced, no
  room, no accent, no hesitation.
- **Whether model confidence deserves a weight above zero.** The signal runs and
  reports; the evidence to raise it has to come from real speech, and there is
  none yet.
- **German entity detection.** Title markers and acronyms are all German gets,
  and neither has been exercised on real German speech.
